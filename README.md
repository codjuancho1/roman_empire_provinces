#Roman_Empire_Provinces_GIS
Roman provincial divisions (represented as lines and polygons), spanning from the early Republic to the conquest of Mesopotamia under Septimius Severus; the dataset is optimized for use in QGIS as a geopackage. Both the “provincias_romanas_l” (lines) and “provincias_romanas_p” (polygons) files follow the same format, with chronological divisions for both the Republic and the Principate. Each stage is further subdivided into shorter periods (for the Republic) or dynasties (for the Principate). The intention is for the corresponding periods in both layers to be activated simultaneously, ensuring they align chronologically. For certain provinces—such as the Alpes Graiae and the Alpes Poeninae—the dates of their creation are a subject of debate. In cases of significant discrepancy, a specific date has been selected, as it is necessary to establish a precise moment for the province to appear in at least one of the sub-periods. To define the boundaries of each province, we relied on mountain ranges, drainage basins, and river courses. The layers used for this delimitation include Esri’s “World Hillshade” (accessible directly within QGIS) and the Copernicus dataset titled “EU-Hydro River Network Database 2006-2012 (vector), Europe” (easily found via an online search). Furthermore, Roman cities from the period were individually verified to ensure they were correctly placed within their respective provinces—a step particularly important for cities located near borders. Given that the exact path of a provincial boundary is sometimes impossible to determine, we prioritized ensuring that Roman cities are included within their appropriate provinces. Within the attribute table of the `roman_provinces_p` polygon layer, there are several columns:
• `fid`: A column containing a unique identification number for each spatial feature, assigned automatically by QGIS.
• `latin`: The Latin name of the province in question.
• `castellano`: The Spanish name of the province in question.
• `creation`: A text field indicating the years during which the province was created. This serves merely as a reference; since it is a text-string field, it cannot be used for calculations.
• `creation_start`: The year the province was first created (“creation start”). In most cases, this coincides with the year shown in the following field.
• `creation_end`: The year the creation of the province was completed, or the year from which there is definitive evidence of its existence (“creation end”).
For example, for the province of “Epirus,” the `creation_start` field shows the year 103 (103 AD) and the `creation_end` field shows 114 (114 AD); while the exact date of the province’s creation is unknown, it is established that it occurred within this timeframe.
The visualization style is structured so that provinces created through the division of older ones—for instance, Pannonia Inferior and Pannonia Superior, which were formed by splitting the territory of the province of Pannonia (with the combined boundaries of the two new provinces matching the original, undivided province)—are layered on top of the earlier ones. This avoids the unnecessary duplication of spatial features while preserving the specific creation dates for each province.
The `.shp` file `roman_provinces_l` follows the same logic. However, in this case, the names do not refer to a single province, but rather to the two provinces separated by the specific line segment (e.g., Gallia Lugdunensis/Gallia Narbonensis). As a final note, the best approach is to combine these provincial boundaries with the city layers provided by the Ancient World Mapping Center (AWMC) at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, as well as the extraordinary Roman road mapping work carried out by the Itiner-e project. Both sources offer their data openly and free of charge on their respective websites. Also noteworthy is the excellent mapping of Roman roads and locations produced by the Digital Britannia project, which likewise provides open-access data.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the most accurate provincial division currently available for this period; however, any corrections are welcome. We are, of course, deeply grateful for the immense collective effort of the cartographers, historians, and Roman history enthusiasts who have made it possible to refine these provincial boundaries to this level of detail. As time permits, we intend to gradually compile a separate "readme" file containing the full bibliography used throughout the creation of these datasets.
